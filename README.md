ear-End Office Cakes in Singapore: The 9-Point Checklist Before You Place the Order
Every December the same email lands in someone's inbox: "Can you organise a cake for the office party?" It sounds like a five-minute task and turns into two weeks of chasing. This is the checklist that prevents that.

1. Who is actually eating it?
An office of 25 people does not need a 3kg cake. A 2kg cake cuts into roughly 20 dessert slices, and in an office at least a third of the room takes a smaller piece or skips it. Order for 70–80% of headcount and you will have enough, not a week of leftovers.

2. Halal status, before anything else.
In a Singapore office this is rarely optional — a mixed team will include colleagues for whom a non-halal cake is not suitable. Ask for the certificate, not the assurance. It should name the premises and cover the products you are ordering, and it should be current.

3. Dietary edge cases you will be asked about.
Eggless, less sugar, no nuts. Less-sugar cakes are the most common request now, and they work best when the flavour is carrying the cake — pandan, ondeh ondeh, cheng tng, yam, gula melaka — rather than a plain sponge with the sugar removed.

4. One cake or individual boxes?
This is the decision that actually changes your budget. A single large cake is cheaper per head but needs plates, a knife, a room, and 20 minutes of everyone standing around. Individual boxes or mini cakes cost more per head and remove all of that — no cutting, no plates, no queue, and they double as the gift.

5. Delivery window vs meeting window.
A cake delivered at 10am for a 3pm party has to survive a fridge, an aircon room, and possibly a reception desk. Ask for a delivery slot inside two hours of serving, and confirm who signs for it — the failure mode here is a cake sitting in a mailroom.

6. Branding.
Printed logos, custom messages and ribbon colours are the part with lead time. Assume two weeks minimum for anything printed, longer in December when every bakery is queued.

7. Invoicing.
Most offices need a proper invoice with a UEN and payment terms, not a receipt. Confirm this before the order is placed, because a bakery that only takes cards at the counter is the wrong supplier for a corporate purchase.

8. The second order nobody plans for.
Someone will be out of office on the party day, or a client will visit the week after. A small set of shelf-stable boxes in the pantry solves it more cheaply than a second delivery.

9. Book the date, not the cake.
In the last three weeks of December the constraint is production slots, not menu choice. Locking the date first and finalising flavours a few days later is how you avoid being told "we are full".
